# Settleye submission post kit

Drafts for LinkedIn, X, and short captions. Copy what fits. Keep the modeled label on Tier 3. Do not claim a win, a customer, or merchant savings.

## One-liner

## LinkedIn, long

I just submitted my first hackathon project.

It is called Settleye. The problem: your AI support agent tells the customer the refund is processed. The ticket closes. The ledger says $0.00 moved.

The agent is not lying. It just never checked.

Settleye reads the transcript, extracts the binding promise, checks Stripe or Shopify ledger state, and shows the gap. One click fixes the shortfall against the test ledger with a signed, idempotent receipt. A retry returns the same receipt and moves $0.00. The demo runs with no install and no API key.

The film is three minutes, with my own voiceover. The sample inbox is live. The repo is public.

What surprised me: the marketing was part of the build. The landing page, the film, the thumbnail, and the submission copy all had to agree on the same proof hierarchy. The hard part was not adding features. It was deciding what to claim, what to label as modeled, and what to leave out.

I used OpenCode with a captain and teamwork loop: `/goal` for the outcome, `/plan` for the task graph, `/teamwork` for parallel work. The workflow helped, but the real lesson was that a demo is an argument. Build the argument, then build the product around it.

## Build-log post

I built Settleye for my first hackathon. The workflow change that mattered: `/goal`, `/plan`, and `/teamwork` in OpenCode. `/goal` fixed the outcome. `/plan` broke it into tasks. `/teamwork` ran independent work in parallel.

The bigger change was treating marketing as a build artifact. The proof hierarchy came first: one live catch, one labeled eval, one modeled fleet. Then the landing page, the three-minute film, the thumbnail, and the submission form all used the same numbers.

The hard part was deciding what not to claim. The 4.0% failure rate is an assumption. The fleet number is modeled. The live catch is one test-mode order. Saying that plainly made the project stronger, not weaker.

## Proof points, with labels

- Tier 1, live and read-only: $44.00 at risk on one Shopify test-mode order #1001, caught in 4.07 seconds. $6.00 settled of a $50.00 promise. No money moved. One order, not a fleet sample.
- Tier 2, labeled eval: 12 of 12 verdicts correct, 11 of 12 entities exact, 0 of 8 binding cases missed, 0 of 4 non-binding promises drifted. $285.50 at risk across 5 drifts.
- Tier 3, modeled not measured: $124,328.40 / $1,256,604.96 / $12,557,168.40 in modeled annual exposure protected at 10,000 / 100,000 / 1,000,000 tickets per month. Assumptions: 10.6% refund intent, 66.7% binding commitment, 4.0% execution failure, $52.50 average refund, 4.61x dispute cost, $128 chargeback fee. The 4.0% failure rate is an assumption, not a merchant measurement. Not a guarantee or a merchant result.
- Checks: 235 backend and 93 frontend.

- A win or placement. The submission is not a result.
- Real merchant savings or live payment claims.
- The fleet projection without `modeled, not measured`.
- The 4.0% failure rate as a merchant measurement.
- A customer name or logo that did not agree to be named.
